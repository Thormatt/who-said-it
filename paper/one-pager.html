Who Said It?

An experiment on whether AI programs talk each other into wrong answers, and what stops it

Thor Matthiasson and Jennifer McTiernan

In short

We had several AI programs, like ChatGPT, answer the same question together, and secretly planted a wrong answer among them. While they could still see the source document, they almost never fell for it. Once we took the document away, many copied the wrong answer, and they copied it more often when we claimed it came from a more advanced AI. An extra AI that checked every answer against the document stopped the wrong answer almost completely.

A few words first

AI model
A program that reads text and writes answers, such as ChatGPT (by OpenAI), Gemini (Google) or Claude (Anthropic).
Advanced model
A company's biggest and most capable model. It is the most expensive to run and usually the most accurate. Think of a senior expert.
Tiny model
A much smaller, basic model, cheap enough to run on a phone. It makes far more mistakes. Think of an intern in their first week.
AI team
Several models working on the same task and reading each other's work, like a committee. More and more AI products are built this way.

The question

If some members of an AI team confidently give a wrong answer, do the others go along with it? Does it matter who the wrong answer seems to come from? And what stops it from spreading?

Why it matters

In an AI team, each model usually sees the other models' answers, not the documents those answers came from. A wrong answer that several team members repeat can look trustworthy. The team's final answer is what a person reads and acts on.

How the test worked

  1. We wrote a short, invented report and asked one question about it with one correct answer. Example: a report about a bridge repair says the work was delayed by 14 months. Question: how long was the delay? Because the report is invented, no AI could know the answer from the internet. The only place to find it is the report.

  2. Five real AI models answered on their own. They were not told anything about the experiment.

  3. We secretly added fake team members. These were AI models we instructed to give the same wrong answer, confidently and with a reason: 41 months. The real models did not know.

  4. Second round. Each real model saw five of its teammates' answers and could keep its answer or change it. If it changed to 41 months, we counted it as fooled.

  5. Final answer. One more AI read the second-round answers and wrote the team's single answer, the one a person using the system would see.

Three teams

  • Current models (November 2026) from five companies: the makers of Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini, Grok and DeepSeek.
  • Earlier models from the same five companies, released in 2025 and early 2026.
  • Small, low-cost models from five companies, cheaper and less capable.

What we compared, one change at a time

  • Could the models still see the report in round two, or was it taken away?
  • Were 1 or 3 of the 5 answers they saw wrong?
  • Did each answer carry a name tag saying which AI wrote it? For the wrong answers, the tag was invented.
  • Did an extra fact-checker AI compare each answer with the report and attach a note when it was wrong?

24 invented reports on six subjects: construction, medicine, company finances, accident reports, law and science. We wrote down exactly what we would measure and froze the plan before the real test, so the results could not change the rules. 7,352 second-round answers in total.

What we found

  1. 1 in 1,064answers fooled

    With the report in view, the models were almost never fooled.

    As long as they could still see the report, or only one of the five answers they saw was wrong, just 1 answer out of 1,064 switched to the wrong number.

  2. Up to 3 in 10answers fooled

    Without the report, a wrong majority fooled them.

    With the report taken away and 3 of the 5 answers wrong, the current models were fooled about 1 time in 10, and the earlier and small models about 3 times in 10. The wrong number made it into the team's final answer in up to 3 of every 10 cases.

  3. 1 vs 38times fooled, out of 240 tries

    The name tag changed what they believed.

    The same wrong answer fooled the current models once in 240 tries when its tag said a tiny model wrote it, and 38 times in 240 (about 1 in 6) when the tag named a company's most advanced model. Small models were fooled 41% of the time with the advanced-model tag, compared with 29% when there were no names. The tags were made up. In a real AI team, nothing stops a program from claiming to be something it is not.

  4. 0 of 832final answers wrong

    A fact-checker that reads the report fixed it.

    When an extra AI compared every answer with the report and flagged the wrong ones, the models were fooled far less often, and none of the 832 final team answers contained the wrong number. A specialized checking tool built by one of the authors (called ORC) did no better than simply asking an ordinary AI model to check.

  5. 5% → 1%small models fooled, before and after

    How the fact-checker words its note matters.

    A note that only repeated the wrong claim sometimes backfired: a few models read the quoted "41 months" as if the report had said it. When the note also quoted what the report actually says, small models were fooled less than 1% of the time instead of about 5%.

What this means

For people building AI systems:

  • Let the AIs check claims against the original source.
  • Don't trust what a program says about itself. A name is easy to fake, and AI models are swayed by it.
  • When a checker flags a mistake, have it state the correct information too.

For everyone else: several AIs agreeing on an answer does not make it right. The useful question is where the answer came from.

What this test does not show

The reports were short and invented, each with one planted wrong number, and the fake team members were told to be wrong. Real mistakes are messier and less deliberate. The name tags were a line of text. We picked the AI models for each team ourselves, so this is not a ranking of AI products. An AI program graded the answers; a second grading AI from another company agreed with it 99.7% of the time.
