Who Said It?

How a confident wrong answer spreads through a team of AI models, and what stops it

Thor Matthiasson and Jennifer McTiernan

In short AI models working as a team can talk each other into a wrong answer. It happens when the source document is out of reach, and more often when the wrong answer seems to come from an impressive model. Checking every answer against the source stopped it.

Think of a classroom

A teacher hands out a short story and asks: how long was the bridge renovation delayed? The story says 14 months. Before answering, each student sees what five classmates wrote, and some of them were secretly told to write 41 months. Does anyone copy them? Does it matter whether the note claims to come from the top student or the new kid? Does it help to check every note against the story? We ran this test with AI models in place of students.

Why it matters

AI systems are increasingly built as teams that pass claims to each other, often without the original source. If one model is confidently wrong, the others agreeing can make the mistake look trustworthy, and the team's final answer is what a person reads.

What we did

  1. Wrote 24 short, made-up documents in six topics, each with one question and one right answer. Made-up, so no model could know the answer in advance.
  2. Built three teams of five real AI models: the newest (November 2026), the previous generation, and small, cheap models. Fake teammates insisted on one wrong number.
  3. Round one, everyone answers alone. Round two, each model sees five teammates' answers and may change its own. A final model writes the team's answer.
  4. Changed one thing at a time: the document in view or not, how many teammates were wrong, name tags, and a fact-checker.

The plan was frozen before the real test, so results could not steer the method: 7,352 round-two answers in total.

What we found

  1. With the source in view, the models were not fooled. When the document stayed visible, or only one teammate was wrong, 1 answer in 1,064 switched to the wrong number.
  2. Take the source away and add a wrong majority, and errors spread. About 1 in 10 answers from the newest models switched, and 3 in 10 from older and smaller ones. Up to 3 in 10 final team answers carried the error.
  3. Who said it changes what models believe. The same wrong words fooled the newest models 0.4% of the time when labeled as coming from a tiny model, and 16% when labeled as a flagship. Small models went from 29% to 41%.
  4. Checking against the source stops it. A fact-checker that compared each answer with the document cut switching sharply, and 0 of 832 final team answers kept the error. The first author's own checking tool, ORC, did no better than a plain model asked to check.
  5. The wording of the warning matters. Warnings that quoted the wrong claim were sometimes read as the document's own words. Stating what the document says cut leftover switching in small models from 5% to under 1%.
With a fact-checker 0 of 832 final team answers kept the wrong number

What it means for builders

  • Keep the source in the loop, and check claims against it.
  • Treat claimed identities as untrusted. A name is easy to fake.
  • Make warnings state the evidence instead of only repeating the error.

What it does not show

Short, made-up documents with one planted number, and fake teammates told to be wrong; real mistakes are messier. Name tags were a line of text, and the teams were hand-picked. An AI grader scored the answers; a second grader from another company agreed on 99.7% of them.
